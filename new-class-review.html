1. Why This Class

Of the 51,917 people in GDC custody, about 9,700 are under 30, nearly one in five (GDC's weekly Friday report, October 16, 2026). Many of them are serving sentences longer than they have been alive. A 20-year sentence for a 20-year-old is a lifetime. Almost a fifth of the people in GDC custody are serving life, life without parole or a death sentence (GDC's monthly inmate profile, October 2026).

When the end is that far away, it's easy to believe nothing you do inside matters. Too many young men decide there's no reason to do good. This class is built to answer that belief.

Education is the clearest place to start. Of the men whose school level GDC has on record, about half never finished 12th grade or earned a GED. A GED is the first door. College is the next one, and it is offered inside: Ashland University's college program in Georgia prisons began at Dooly State Prison in GDC's 2019 fiscal year, and in fiscal 2023 GDC reported 38 bachelor's and 87 associate degrees earned by men and women in its custody.

FORGE already trains mentors. This class gives those mentors the men who most need them, and it gives younger men a reason to start growing now instead of waiting.

2. What This Class Is, and What It Isn't

It is:

  • A short class for men under 30: about 12 weeks, one or two hours a week.
  • A starting point: thinking about the future, getting a GED, then getting into college or a trade program, and building a record that shows growth.
  • On the record. Completions of this class are entered in a man's GDC record (SCRIBE), the same as FORGE completions.
  • Membership, not just a class. Graduates stay connected through a monthly members' meeting and a FORGE mentor.
  • A way to change a dorm from the inside, one man at a time.

It isn't:

  • The FORGE mentor certification program. It's shorter, lighter and asks less. Graduates who want to go further can apply for mentor training later.
  • Therapy, a religious program, or a substitute for school.
  • A promise of parole or early release. See Section 4.

4. The Message of Hope: What We Say and What We Never Say

This is the most important section of the plan. The young men we want will test every promise we make, and they will ask older men whether it's true. If one promise turns out to be false, the class loses that man and every man he talks to.

What the law says today

If the sentence is… Parole under today's law What growth can still do
Most other crimes (drugs, burglary, theft, aggravated assault and so on) Parole-eligible. The Parole Board sets a Tentative Parole Month that depends on good conduct and sometimes on finishing a program. It bears directly on whether that month holds.
A set number of years for one of the seven serious violent felonies: murder, armed robbery, kidnapping, rape, aggravated child molestation, aggravated sodomy, aggravated sexual battery Served in full. No parole and no earned time. In his last year, he can be considered for a transitional center or work release. The transitional center in the last year. Security level, transfers and job assignments. First in line if the law changes.
Life for one of those seven crimes First parole consideration after 30 years. The Board will read 30 years of his file.
Life without parole None under today's law. Only a change in the law would open a door. If one comes, the file is what will count.

Source: O.C.G.A. § 17-10-6.1; State Board of Pardons and Paroles. Some other sentences carry their own no-parole rules (for example, some repeat-offender sentences). When a man isn't sure where he stands, help him ask his counselor.

Laws do change

Other states have changed their laws to reward exactly what this class builds. California, for example, now gives time credits for education and rehabilitation programs, and gives earlier parole hearings to people who were under 26 when they committed their crime. Nobody can say whether or when Georgia will do something like it. But when laws change, the men who benefit first are the ones whose record shows they grew.

What we say

Nobody can promise you a law will change. But laws do change. When they do, the men who go first are the ones whose file shows they grew. Start building yours now. And if the law never changes, a GED, college credits and a clean record still make you a different man, inside or out.

What we never say

  • "FORGE will get you parole" or "This will get you out early."
  • "They're about to let people out," or any timeline at all.
  • Anything about a particular man's case or his chances. Send those questions to his counselor or a lawyer.

What's in a man's file

A GED, college credits, work assignments, programs completed, and his disciplinary record all go into his GDC record. Completions of this class will be entered in SCRIBE, GDC's record system, the same as FORGE completions. So a man can be told plainly: finishing this class goes in your file, and so do the GED, the college credits and the clean record that come with it.

When a man says "I've got life without parole. Why bother?"

  1. Laws change, and the men with a record are first in line when they do.
  2. However long he's here, who he is for those years is his to decide.
  3. Men serving long sentences become the leaders of a dorm. The younger men watch them.

5. Goals: What a Man Leaves With

By the end of the 12 weeks, each man has:

  1. Signed up for GED classes or testing. If he already has a GED, he has applied to a college or trade program, or is helping another man with his GED.
  2. A written 10-year plan, with the next step for this month.
  3. Twelve weeks of clean conduct. A goal, not a requirement.
  4. A FORGE mentor he can go to.
  5. One thing he does for someone else in his dorm.

6. Format

Option Shape Total
A. Recommended Two 6-week parts, two 1-hour meetings a week 24 hours
B 12 straight weeks, two 1-hour meetings a week 24 hours
C Two 6-week parts, one 90-minute meeting a week 18 hours
D 12 straight weeks, one 90-minute meeting a week 18 hours

Why Option A:

  • Part 1 is a small first ask: six weeks, not twelve. A man can say yes to six weeks.
  • A quick win: a certificate and a GED sign-up at week 6.
  • Part 2 is his choice. The men who come back have chosen to.
  • An hour holds a young man better than two. Twice a week builds a habit, and missing one meeting doesn't lose a whole week.
  • The second meeting each week is the working one: GED study check, goals, time with a mentor.
  • A new Part 1 group can start every 6 weeks while the previous group does Part 2.

Men arrive one at a time. A man can start only after he moves into the FORGE dorm, and beds open when other men move out. That happens fairly regularly, but not on a schedule. So the plan has to say how a new man starts.

  • Working time (GED study check, the 10-year plan, reading): 30 minutes

10. Getting Men to Join

This is the hardest part. Ideas, strongest first:

  1. Flyers in every dorm. Five samples are below, under Samples. Each one tells a man how to get an application. Replace them when they get torn down.
  2. The messengers. Older FORGE men who were 20 once. Their own words go on a flyer (Flyer 5) and in a short letter handed out with the applications. Men in the FORGE dorm tell the young men they know in other dorms: at chow, on the yard, at work and at school.
  3. Counselors. Counselors in each dorm can point young men on their caseload to the class.
  4. Ask first. Hand out the interest survey (see Samples, below) with the first flyers. Men join what they helped choose.
  5. A small first ask. Six weeks, a certificate, a GED sign-up, and a completion entered in his GDC record.
  6. Membership. Something to belong to.
  7. Topics they want. The money-and-work week will draw men who would skip a class about character.
  8. Each graduate brings one.
  9. Things only staff can grant (Section 15): ask for them, but never promise them until they're granted.
Your callRecruiting

11. Joining, Step by Step

  1. A man sees a flyer in his dorm, or hears about the class.
  2. He gets an application, fills it out (see Samples, below) and turns it in.
  3. FORGE mentors read it, and if possible one of them talks with him for about 10 minutes: why he wants in, what he hopes to get, and the commitments.
  4. FORGE mentors and the chief counselor approve the application, or turn it down.
  5. The chief counselor moves him into the FORGE dorm when a bed opens. Beds open when men move out, which happens fairly regularly.
  6. He starts with the next group (Section 6) and signs the member pledge at his first meeting.

14. What We'll Track

  • Who joins and who finishes each part
  • GED sign-ups and GED passes
  • College and trade-program applications
  • Disciplinary reports: the six months before a man joins, compared with during and after

Only with each man's permission, and only as numbers, never names, in any report. These numbers are what will persuade staff to keep and grow the class. They're also the only honest basis for ever saying that FORGE helps at parole.

15. Questions Only Staff Can Answer

Before the first group starts, someone needs to ask:

  1. Beds. How many FORGE dorm beds can men in this class hold at one time? (Chief counselor.)
  2. After the class. Does a graduate stay in the FORGE dorm? Peer Leaders and the monthly members' meeting depend on it.
  3. The record. Completions of this class will be entered in SCRIBE, as FORGE completions are. Who enters them, and how soon after a man finishes? Does finishing Part 1 alone get entered, or only the whole class?
  4. College and GED. Which college programs (Ashland or others) and GED classes are open to men here now, and how does a man enroll?
  5. Certificates. Can a copy go in a man's file?
  6. Flyers. Who posts them in each dorm, and who replaces them?
  7. Graduation. Can family be invited?

About FORGE

FORGE is a peer-led, evidence-based mentorship program designed to build a safer, more stable dorm culture by training men to lead through service, accountability, and conflict reduction.

"To turn time served into time that serves others."
